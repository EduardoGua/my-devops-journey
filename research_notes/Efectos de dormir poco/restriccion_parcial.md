# Partial (chronic) sleep restriction in humans: dose (3-6 h/night) x time (days-weeks) vs 7-9 h

Scope: experimental human studies of chronic partial sleep restriction (PSR), plus a few key observational and immune studies. Unless stated otherwise, "4 h", "5 h" and similar are **time in bed (TIB)**. Actual sleep is usually somewhat less.

Source access note: PubMed abstracts were retrieved through NCBI E-utilities, and some full texts through PMC. A number not seen in a primary text is flagged as coming from a press release or as unverified.

---

## Q1. What changes after 1 week and after 2 weeks at 4, 5 and 6 h? (dose x duration table)

### Takeaway
Neurobehavioral deficits build up night after night in a dose-dependent way. After 14 days, 6 h TIB produces cognitive deficits comparable to about 1 night of total deprivation and 4 h comparable to about 2 nights. Physiological systems react within about 2-9 nights: glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity fall (roughly 10-27%), energy intake rises (about +385 kcal/day pooled), blood pressure rises (especially in women), daytime testosterone falls 10-15%, 711 blood genes are dysregulated, and the antibody response to vaccination is roughly halved. 3 h TIB is the only dose at which PVT performance kept declining steeply and linearly across 7 days.

### Cited findings

**Neurobehavioral dose-response**
- Van Dongen et al. 2003. n=48 adults aged 21-38, randomized to 4, 6 or 8 h TIB for 14 consecutive days, compared with 0 h for 3 days. 4 h and 6 h produced "significant cumulative, dose-dependent deficits in cognitive performance on all tasks." Chronic restriction to 6 h or less produced deficits "equivalent to up to 2 nights of total sleep deprivation." — [Van Dongen HP, Maislin G, Mullington JM, Dinges DF. The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness... Sleep. 2003;26(2):117-26. doi:10.1093/sleep/26.2.117](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117)
- Van Dongen 2003: PVT lapses were "near-linearly related to the cumulative duration of wakefulness in excess of 15.84 h (s.e. 0.73 h)" regardless of how sleep was lost. Polysomnography and NREM delta power showed an acute response with "negligible further changes across the 14 restricted nights," so sleep physiology did not track the growing cognitive deficit. — [Van Dongen 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117)
- Belenky et al. 2003. n=66, 3 h (n=18), 5 h (16), 7 h (16) or 9 h (16) TIB for 7 days, then 3 days of 8 h. At 3 h, PVT speed declined and lapses increased steadily. At 5 h and 7 h, speed fell, then stabilized lower; lapses increased only at 5 h. 9 h stayed at baseline. The authors propose that "the brain adapts to chronic sleep restriction," stabilizing performance at a reduced level. — [Belenky G, Wesensten NJ, Thorne DR, et al. Patterns of performance degradation and restoration during sleep restriction and subsequent recovery: a sleep dose-response study. J Sleep Res. 2003;12(1):1-12. doi:10.1046/j.1365-2869.2003.00337.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2869.2003.00337.x)
- Note that 7 h TIB (not only 5 h) produced a measurable slowing of PVT speed in Belenky, so 7 h TIB was not fully "free" in this sample. — [Belenky 2003](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2869.2003.00337.x)
- Dinges et al. 1997. n=16, sleep cut 33% to 4.98 h (SD 0.57) for 7 nights. Cumulative increases in sleepiness (SSS), POMS fatigue, confusion, tension and total mood disturbance, plus VAS mental exhaustion and stress (p=0.009 to 0.0001). PVT lapse frequency and duration increased. Changes were largest on days 1-2 and 6-7, and "steady-state sleepiness was not achieved." — [Dinges DF, Pack F, Williams K, et al. Cumulative sleepiness, mood disturbance, and psychomotor vigilance performance decrements during a week of sleep restricted to 4-5 hours per night. Sleep. 1997;20(4):267-77. PMID 9231952](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9231952/)

**Metabolic and appetite**
- Spiegel, Leproult & Van Cauter 1999. n=11 young men, 4 h TIB for 6 nights compared with 12 h TIB for 6 nights. Lower glucose tolerance (p<0.02), lower TSH (p<0.01), higher evening cortisol (p=0.0001), higher sympathetic activity (p<0.02). The authors note the effects are "similar to those seen in normal ageing." — [Spiegel K, Leproult R, Van Cauter E. Impact of sleep debt on metabolic and endocrine function. Lancet. 1999;354(9188):1435-9. doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(99)01376-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(99)01376-8)
- Press release on the same study (secondary source; I did not see these numbers in the abstract): glucose took about 40% longer to return to baseline; insulin secretion and response down about 30%; glucose tolerance "consistent with current diagnostic criteria for impaired glucose tolerance"; abnormalities "quickly returned to baseline during the recovery period" of 12 h nights. — [UChicago Medicine, Oct 1999](https://www.uchicagomedicine.org/forefront/news/1999/october/lack-of-sleep-alters-hormones-metabolism-simulates-effects-of-aging)
- Buxton et al. 2010. n=20 men, 10 h TIB, then 5 h TIB for 7 nights. Insulin sensitivity down 20 ± 24% (IVGTT, p=0.001) and 11 ± 5.5% (clamp, p<0.04). Glucose tolerance and disposition index reduced. Salivary cortisol +51 ± 8%. Modafinil did not prevent the changes. — [Buxton OM, Pavlova M, Reid EW, et al. Sleep restriction for 1 week reduces insulin sensitivity in healthy men. Diabetes. 2010;59(9):2126-33. doi:10.2337/db09-0699](https://doi.org/10.2337/db09-0699)
- Nedeltcheva et al. 2009. n=11, crossover of two 14-day lab stays at 5.5 h vs 8.5 h TIB with free access to food. Sleep fell 122 ± 25 min per night. Snack calories 1087 ± 541 vs 866 ± 365 kcal/day (p=0.026), carbohydrate share 65% vs 61%, mostly 19:00-07:00. Meals, energy expenditure (2526 vs 2390 kcal/day, p=0.58), leptin and ghrelin did not differ significantly. — [Nedeltcheva AV, Kilkus JM, Imperial J, et al. Sleep curtailment is accompanied by increased intake of calories from snacks. Am J Clin Nutr. 2009;89(1):126-33. doi:10.3945/ajcn.2008.26574](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.2008.26574)
- Spiegel et al. 2004. n=12 men, 2 days of restriction vs extension with controlled intake. Leptin down 18%, ghrelin up 28%, hunger up 24%, appetite up 23%; calorie-dense high-carbohydrate foods up 33-45%. — [Spiegel K, Tasali E, Penev P, Van Cauter E. Sleep curtailment in healthy young men is associated with decreased leptin levels, elevated ghrelin levels, and increased hunger and appetite. Ann Intern Med. 2004;141(11):846-50. doi:10.7326/0003-4819-141-11-200412070-00008](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-141-11-200412070-00008)
- The leptin and ghrelin results conflict: with free access to food, Nedeltcheva 2009 found no leptin or ghrelin difference, and Markwald 2013 found hormone changes that "signaled excess energy stores." Overeating under free access is therefore not well explained by leptin and ghrelin. — [Nedeltcheva 2009](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.2008.26574); [Markwald 2013](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1216951110)
- Markwald et al. 2013. n=16, 5 days of insufficient sleep (5 h opportunity). Total daily energy expenditure +about 5%, but intake (especially after dinner) exceeded needs; weight gain 0.82 ± 0.47 kg; melatonin phase delayed. Returning to adequate sleep reduced intake (weight change -0.03 kg). — [Markwald RR, Melanson EL, Smith MR, et al. Impact of insufficient sleep on total daily energy expenditure, food intake, and weight gain. PNAS. 2013;110(14):5695-700. doi:10.1073/pnas.1216951110](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1216951110)
- Al Khatib et al. 2017 meta-analysis. 17 studies in the review, 11 (n=172) in the meta-analysis. PSR increased energy intake by 385 kcal/day (95% CI 252-517) with no change in total energy expenditure or resting metabolic rate; more fat, less protein. — [Al Khatib HK, Harding SV, Darzi J, Pot GK. The effects of partial sleep deprivation on energy balance: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Eur J Clin Nutr. 2017;71(5):614-24. doi:10.1038/ejcn.2016.201](https://doi.org/10.1038/ejcn.2016.201)
- Covassin et al. 2022. n=12, crossover with 14 days at 4 h vs 9 h sleep opportunity and free access to food. More calories (p=0.015), more weight gain (p=0.008); total fat unchanged, but total abdominal fat increased (p=0.011), both subcutaneous (p=0.047) and visceral (p=0.042). — [Covassin N, Singh P, McCrady-Spitzer SK, et al. Effects of experimental sleep restriction on energy intake, energy expenditure, and visceral obesity. J Am Coll Cardiol. 2022;79(13):1254-65. doi:10.1016/j.jacc.2022.01.038](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2022.01.038)

**Blood pressure and cardiovascular**
- Covassin et al. 2021. n=20 (9 women), crossover with 9 nights at 4 h vs 9 h, then recovery. Day 12: 24-h MAP +2.1 mmHg (95% CI 0.6-3.6), SBP +3.1, DBP +2.2; endothelial function attenuated; norepinephrine up. Only women showed the rise: 24-h SBP +8.0 (5.1-10.8) and sleep-time SBP +11.3 mmHg, with "no signs of adaptation." In women, 24-h SBP stayed +4.1 mmHg into recovery (Day 15). — [Covassin N, Bukartyk J, Singh P, et al. Effects of experimental sleep restriction on ambulatory and sleep blood pressure in healthy young adults: a randomized crossover study. Hypertension. 2021;78(3):859-70. doi:10.1161/HYPERTENSIONAHA.121.17622](https://doi.org/10.1161/HYPERTENSIONAHA.121.17622) (full text: [PMC8363516](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8363516/))
- Makarem et al. 2021. Women, 6 weeks of mild restriction (bedtime delayed 1.5 h) vs habitual sleep. 24-h SBP +2.95 ± 1.20 mmHg (p=0.024), MAP +2.22 (p=0.035); postmenopausal women had sleep SBP +6.27 mmHg (p=0.037). — [Makarem N, Zuraikat FM, Scaccia SE, RoyChoudhury A, St-Onge MP. Sustained mild sleep restriction increases blood pressure in women. Hypertension. 2021;77(5):e50-e52. doi:10.1161/HYPERTENSIONAHA.120.16370](https://doi.org/10.1161/HYPERTENSIONAHA.120.16370)
- Reichenberger et al. 2023. n=15 men, 3 x 10 h, then 5 x 5 h, then 2 x 10 h recovery. During recovery, HR was +5.5 bpm and SBP +2.9 mmHg compared with baseline. Across the study, HR rose 7.6 bpm and SBP 3.4 mmHg. — [Reichenberger DA, Ness KM, Strayer SM, et al. Recovery sleep after sleep restriction is insufficient to return elevated daytime heart rate and systolic blood pressure to baseline levels. Psychosom Med. 2023;85(8):744-51. doi:10.1097/PSY.0000000000001229](https://doi.org/10.1097/PSY.0000000000001229)

**Immune**
- Spiegel, Sheridan & Van Cauter 2002 (JAMA research letter). Young men, 4 h TIB (1-5 AM) for 6 nights, then 12 h recovery nights; influenza vaccination during restriction. Antibody levels at 10 days were about half of controls (figure from UChicago secondary summaries; I did not see the original letter's text). — [Spiegel K, Sheridan JF, Van Cauter E. Effect of sleep deprivation on response to immunization. JAMA. 2002;288(12):1471-2. doi:10.1001/jama.288.12.1471-a](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.288.12.1471-a); [UChicago News summary](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/not-getting-enough-sleep-can-dilute-antibody-response-vaccination-study-finds)
- Prather et al. 2012 (observational). n=125 adults aged 40-60, hepatitis B series. Shorter actigraphy sleep predicted a lower secondary antibody response and lower odds of clinical protection. PMC text: <6 h vs >7 h, OR for protection 0.09 (95% CI 0.01-0.94), i.e. about 11x more likely to be unprotected (press coverage cites "11.5 times"); each extra hour of sleep went with +56% secondary antibodies. Subjective sleep quality was not a predictor. — [Prather AA, Hall M, Fury JM, et al. Sleep and antibody response to hepatitis B vaccination. Sleep. 2012;35(8):1063-9. doi:10.5665/sleep.1990](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.1990); [PMC3397812](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3397812/); [LiveScience coverage](https://www.livescience.com/22023-sleep-vaccine-response.html)
- Prather et al. 2015 (observational, then experimental rhinovirus challenge). n=164; 7 days of actigraphy, then nasal rhinovirus. Compared with >7 h: <5 h OR 4.50 (1.08-18.69); 5-6 h OR 4.24 (1.08-16.71); 6.01-7 h OR 1.66 (0.40-6.95, not significant). Sleep fragmentation was unrelated. — [Prather AA, Janicki-Deverts D, Hall MH, Cohen S. Behaviorally assessed sleep and susceptibility to the common cold. Sleep. 2015;38(9):1353-9. doi:10.5665/sleep.4968](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4968)
- Spiegel et al. 2023 meta-analysis. Objectively measured <6 h around vaccination: effect size 0.79 (0.40-1.18), n=304. Self-reported short sleep was not significant (0.29, -0.04 to 0.63). Men 0.93; women not significant. — [Spiegel K, Rey AE, Cheylus A, et al. A meta-analysis of the associations between insufficient sleep duration and antibody response to vaccination. Curr Biol. 2023;33(5):998-1005.e2. doi:10.1016/j.cub.2023.02.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2023.02.017)

**Gene expression**
- Möller-Levet et al. 2013. n=26, crossover with 1 week at 5.70 h vs 8.50 h sleep. 711 genes up- or down-regulated; circadian genes fell from 1,855 to 1,481 with lower amplitude; genes responsive to later total deprivation rose from 122 to 856. Affected genes included PER1/2/3, CRY2, CLOCK, IL6, STAT3 and GHRL; affected processes included inflammatory, immune, stress and chromatin modification. — [Möller-Levet CS, Archer SN, Bucca G, et al. Effects of insufficient sleep on circadian rhythmicity and expression amplitude of the human blood transcriptome. PNAS. 2013;110(12):E1132-41. doi:10.1073/pnas.1217154110](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1217154110)

**Testosterone and mood**
- Leproult & Van Cauter 2011. n=10 men aged about 24; 3 nights at 10 h, then 8 nights at 5 h TIB (TST 8 h 55 min -> 4 h 48 min). Waking (8 AM-10 PM) testosterone 16.5 vs 18.4 nmol/L (p=0.049); 2-10 PM 15.5 vs 17.9 (p=0.02), so down 10-15%. Cortisol unchanged. Vigor fell from 28 to 19 (p=0.002). Normal aging lowers testosterone 1-2% per year. — [Leproult R, Van Cauter E. Effect of 1 week of sleep restriction on testosterone levels in young healthy men. JAMA. 2011;305(21):2173-4. doi:10.1001/jama.2011.710](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2011.710) (full text: [PMC4445839](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4445839/))

### Inferences
- **About 1 week at 4-5 h** is enough for clinically meaningful metabolic effects (10-27% insulin-sensitivity loss, impaired glucose tolerance), endocrine effects (testosterone down 10-15%, evening cortisol up), immune effects (vaccine response about halved) and transcriptomic effects (711 genes). Most physiological changes appear within 2-9 nights.
- **About 2 weeks at 4-6 h** mainly adds further cognitive deterioration (cumulative, roughly linear in excess wake time; Van Dongen) and body composition change (visceral fat at 4 h for 14 days; Covassin 2022). Physiological sleep markers plateau early, and cognition does not.
- **At 1 week, dose matters for the shape of the decline**: at 3 h, decline is linear with no plateau; at 5-7 h it is an early drop followed by a lowered plateau (Belenky). Van Dongen's 6 h group still worsened over 14 days, so the "plateau" in Belenky's 5-7 h groups may partly reflect only 7 days of observation or task differences. This is my interpretation, not resolved in the sources.
- Immune and cold-risk data at "<6 h" come mostly from **observational** natural-sleep studies (Prather 2012, 2015). They support but do not prove causation; Spiegel 2002 is the main experimental anchor.

### Gaps
- No study found here directly tests **6 h for 1 week** on metabolic, testosterone or immune endpoints; most physiology studies use 4-5 h. Effects at 6 h are mostly inferred from cognition (Van Dongen) and mild-restriction BP (Makarem).
- The exact Van Dongen day-14 equivalence (6 h ≈ 1 night, 4 h ≈ 2 nights of total deprivation) comes from the paper's figures. The abstract says only "up to 2 nights." I did not retrieve the full text.
- The Spiegel 1999 "40% slower glucose clearance / -30% insulin" and Spiegel 2002 "about half antibodies" figures were verified only through University of Chicago press materials, not the original full text.
- The commonly quoted "+300-500 kcal/day" range is consistent with the meta-analytic +385 kcal (95% CI 252-517). Individual studies range from about +221 kcal (Nedeltcheva, snacks only) and up.

## Q2. Is there a threshold? Is recovery complete after 1-3 nights of recovery sleep (including weekend catch-up)?

### Takeaway
The best-supported performance "threshold" is about 15.8 h of continuous wake per day. Lapses rise near-linearly with wake time beyond that, which implies a need for about 8.2 h of sleep per day for no deficit build-up (Van Dongen 2003). Clinical outcome data point to <6-7 h (colds, vaccine response). Recovery from a week of restriction is **not** complete after 1 night, even of 10 h, and often not after 2-3 nights for vigilance, heart rate and BP, and sleep architecture. Weekend catch-up (about +1.1 h total) does not prevent metabolic dysregulation (Depner 2019). Metabolic and appetite measures may normalize faster, but the evidence is mixed.

### Cited findings
- Van Dongen 2003: lapses were "near-linearly related to the cumulative duration of wakefulness in excess of 15.84 h (s.e. 0.73 h)." With a 24-h day, staying under that threshold implies about 8.16 h of sleep per day. — [Van Dongen 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117)
- Belenky 2003: 9 h TIB was the only dose with **no** PVT change; 7 h TIB slowed PVT speed (without extra lapses). — [Belenky 2003](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2869.2003.00337.x)
- Prather 2015: risk of a cold was elevated at <5 h and 5-6 h but **not** at 6.01-7 h (OR 1.66, not significant) compared with >7 h, which suggests an immune-risk inflection around 6 h. — [Prather 2015](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4968)
- Spiegel 2023 meta-analysis used <6 h/night as the cut-off for "insufficient." The authors list "the amount of sleep needed to protect the response" as still undefined. — [Spiegel 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2023.02.017)
- Belenky 2003 recovery (3 x 8 h TIB): the 5 h and 7 h groups showed "no evidence of recovery" in PVT speed (and in lapses for 5 h). The 3 h group improved rapidly after night 1, but "recovery was incomplete," stabilizing at the 5 h and 7 h groups' level. — [Belenky 2003](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2869.2003.00337.x)
- Banks et al. 2010. n=159; 5 nights at 4 h TIB, then 1 recovery night of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 or 10 h. Recovery improved with dose (exponential for PVT and KSS, linear for MWT), but "recovery was incomplete at the 10 h TIB (8.96 h TST) for PVT performance, KSS sleepiness, and POMS fatigue." Complete recovery "may require ... multiple nights." — [Banks S, Van Dongen HP, Maislin G, Dinges DF. Neurobehavioral dynamics following chronic sleep restriction: dose-response effects of one night for recovery. Sleep. 2010;33(8):1013-26. doi:10.1093/sleep/33.8.1013](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/33.8.1013)
- Dinges 1997: recovery "appeared to require two full nights of sleep" (subset data). — [Dinges 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9231952/)
- Depner et al. 2019. Three groups: control 9 h (n=8); SR 5 h for 9 nights (n=14); WR (n=14) = 5 workdays at 5 h, 2 weekend days of free sleep, then 2 more nights at 5 h. Over the weekend, participants slept only about 1.1 h more than baseline in total. After the weekend, circadian phase was delayed and after-dinner intake and weight rose again. Insulin sensitivity fell about 13% in SR; in WR, whole-body, hepatic and muscle insulin sensitivity fell about 9-27%, so WR was not better and was in some tissues worse. Women slept less than men over the weekend. Conclusion: weekend recovery "is not an effective strategy to prevent metabolic dysregulation." — [Depner CM, Melanson EL, Eckel RH, et al. Ad libitum weekend recovery sleep fails to prevent metabolic dysregulation during a repeating pattern of insufficient sleep and weekend recovery sleep. Curr Biol. 2019;29(6):957-67.e4. doi:10.1016/j.cub.2019.01.069](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.01.069)
- Reichenberger 2023: after 5 x 5 h, HR and SBP "did not recover to baseline levels after two recovery nights" (10 h each). — [Reichenberger 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/PSY.0000000000001229)
- Covassin 2021: in women, 24-h SBP was still +4.1 mmHg and sleep SBP +5.7 mmHg on recovery Day 15, after 9 nights at 4 h. — [Covassin 2021, PMC8363516](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8363516/)
- Ochab et al. 2021 (natural setting). 10 days of 30% restriction relative to individual need, then 7 days of recovery. Only Stroop mean reaction time returned to baseline; EEG, actigraphy and other measures did not, so "a week of recovery ... is insufficient to recover fully." — [Ochab JK, Szwed J, Oleś K, et al. Observing changes in human functioning during induced sleep deficiency and recovery periods. PLoS One. 2021;16(9):e0255771. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0255771](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0255771)
- Ong et al. 2016 (adolescents aged 15-19). 7 nights at 5 h TIB, then 3 nights at 9 h. REM, N2 and TST were still above baseline on the 3rd recovery night, i.e. sleep debt was not yet repaid. — [Ong JL, Lo JC, Gooley JJ, Chee MW. EEG changes across multiple nights of sleep restriction and recovery in adolescents: the Need for Sleep study. Sleep. 2016;39(6):1233-40. doi:10.5665/sleep.5840](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.5840)
- In contrast, Spiegel 1999's metabolic and endocrine abnormalities "quickly returned to baseline during the recovery period," but that recovery was 6-7 nights of **12 h TIB**, not 1-3 nights. — [UChicago press release](https://www.uchicagomedicine.org/forefront/news/1999/october/lack-of-sleep-alters-hormones-metabolism-simulates-effects-of-aging); [Spiegel 1999](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(99)01376-8)
- Markwald 2013: switching to adequate or recovery sleep reduced energy intake (fats and carbohydrates), and weight was roughly stable (-0.03 kg). Appetite responds quickly, but the weight already gained was not lost. — [Markwald 2013](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1216951110)

### Inferences
- No sharp single threshold appears; the evidence fits a **continuous dose-response** in which deficits accumulate whenever daily sleep falls below individual need (about 8 h TIB in lab samples). Clinically relevant outcomes (colds, vaccine response, BP) show clearer harm below about 6 h. 7 h TIB is close to, but not entirely at, zero deficit in some lab data.
- Recovery is dose- and time-dependent: 1 night is never enough after ≥5 nights at 4-5 h. 2-3 nights at 8-10 h partially restore subjective sleepiness, but vigilance, BP/HR and sleep architecture often lag. Belenky's results suggest a slow "adaptive" component that persists for days.
- A typical weekday 5 h / weekend catch-up pattern does not erase the metabolic cost, partly because people recover only about 1 h in total on the weekend and circadian phase shifts.

### Gaps
- Few studies follow recovery past 3 nights in a controlled lab (Ochab 2021 is natural-setting with 7 days). The time course to *full* recovery after 2 weeks at 4-6 h is unknown.
- Belenky's 7 h group not recovering on 8 h TIB is hard to interpret without the full text (it may partly reflect practice or learning effects or an adaptation). I did not verify this.
- Individual differences (trait vulnerability, sex, age) are large but were not quantified here, apart from the sex differences in BP (Covassin 2021), vaccine response (Spiegel 2023) and weekend sleep (Depner 2019).

## Q3. Does subjective sleepiness track actual impairment?

### Takeaway
No, not reliably. Over 14 days, subjective sleepiness rose in the first days and then nearly plateaued, and it did not separate 4 h from 6 h, while objective cognitive deficits kept accumulating. Van Dongen et al. concluded subjects were "largely unaware" of their growing impairment. After recovery, subjective sleepiness may also normalize before vigilance does. One exception: in a 7-day study at about 5 h (Dinges 1997), subjective sleepiness did accumulate alongside PVT lapses and even preceded them by about 1 day.

### Cited findings
- Van Dongen 2003: "Subjective sleepiness ratings showed an acute response to sleep restriction but only small further increases on subsequent days, and did not significantly differentiate the 6 h and 4 h conditions." Sleepiness ratings "suggest that subjects were largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits, which may explain why the impact of chronic sleep restriction on waking cognitive functions is often assumed to be benign." — [Van Dongen 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117)
- Dinges 1997: SSS sleepiness and POMS fatigue and confusion increased cumulatively across 7 nights at about 5 h. "Subjective changes tended to precede performance changes by 1 day," and "steady-state sleepiness was not achieved." — [Dinges 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9231952/)
- Banks 2010: after 5 x 4 h and 1 recovery night, KSS subjective sleepiness and PVT recovery followed exponential dose-response functions; both remained incomplete at 10 h TIB. — [Banks 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/33.8.1013)
- Leproult 2011: subjective vigor fell steadily from 28 to 19 over the 7 nights at 5 h, so some subjective measures do keep tracking the accumulating debt. — [Leproult & Van Cauter 2011, PMC4445839](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4445839/)
- Physiological sleep markers (NREM delta power, polysomnography) also "did not mirror waking neurobehavioral responses" during chronic restriction. — [Van Dongen 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117)
- Subjective sleep measures also predict immune outcomes worse than objective ones: in the vaccine meta-analysis, self-reported short sleep was not significant (effect size 0.29) while objectively measured short sleep was (0.79). In Prather 2012, subjective sleep quality did not predict antibody response. — [Spiegel 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2023.02.017); [Prather 2012](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.1990)

### Inferences
- Over short restriction (≤1 week at about 4-5 h), subjective sleepiness rises in parallel with impairment. Over longer restriction (2 weeks), it saturates while objective deficits keep growing, so the gap between felt and actual impairment **widens with time**. People who restrict chronically to 6 h are therefore poor judges of their own impairment.
- People also misjudge how much they sleep (self-report vs actigraphy), which adds a second layer of misperception. This is my inference from the self-report vs objective differences in the immune studies.

### Gaps
- I found no primary data quantifying the correlation between subjective sleepiness and PVT lapses at day 14 versus day 7 in the same subjects, beyond Van Dongen's qualitative statement.
- I did not retrieve the original Van Dongen figures for the exact Stanford Sleepiness Scale trajectories.

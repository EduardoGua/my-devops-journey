# Long-term (habitual) health consequences of short sleep in humans: epidemiology and meta-analyses

Scope: habitual or chronic short sleep (<6 h, <5 h) compared with about 7-8 h, over months to years. Acute and experimental effects are covered only where they support causality (for example amyloid after one night, or drowsy-driving crash risk).

## What are the best-quantified long-term risks and their magnitudes?

### Takeaway
The best-quantified risks of habitual short sleep are modest relative risks, mostly 1.1 to 1.5. The most robust are:
- all-cause mortality (+12%)
- coronary heart disease (+48%)
- obesity (OR 1.55 in adults, 1.89 in children)
- type 2 diabetes (+9% per hour below 7 h)
- incident hypertension (+7% overall, +11% at <5 h)
- depression (+31%)
- dementia when short sleep occurs in midlife (+22% to +37%)

The largest effect sizes are acute and come from drowsy driving: 4-5 h of sleep gives about 4.3x the crash risk, and <4 h about 11.5x. Overall cancer risk shows no consistent association with sleep duration. Night shift work, however, is classified by IARC as Group 2A, "probably carcinogenic".

### Cited findings

**All-cause mortality**
- Cappuccio FP, D'Elia L, Strazzullo P, Miller MA. "Sleep duration and all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies." *Sleep* 2010;33(5):585-592. DOI: 10.1093/sleep/33.5.585 — [PMC2864873](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864873/)
  - Pooled results: short sleep RR 1.12 (1.06-1.18), long sleep RR 1.30 (1.22-1.38).
  - Data: 16 studies, 27 cohorts, 1,382,999 participants, 112,566 deaths, 4-25 years of follow-up.
  - Sleep was self-reported by questionnaire. No publication bias was found. Heterogeneity was P=0.02 for short sleep and P<0.0001 for long sleep. — [Warwick WRAP](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/5980/)
  - The definition of "short" ranged from <4 h to ≤6 h depending on the study. — [stafforini summary](https://stafforini.com/works/cappuccio-2010-sleep-duration-allcause/)
- Yin J, et al. "Relationship of sleep duration with all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies." *J Am Heart Assoc* 2017;6(9):e005947. DOI: 10.1161/JAHA.117.005947 — [PMC5634263](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634263)
  - All outcomes had a U-shape, with the lowest risk at ≈7 h/day.
  - Mortality: RR 1.06 (1.04-1.07) per hour below 7 h and 1.13 (1.11-1.15) per hour above 7 h.
  - Total CVD: 1.06 per hour below 7 h and 1.12 per hour above.
  - Stroke: 1.05 per hour below 7 h and 1.18 per hour above.
- Kwok CS, et al. *JAHA* 2018, a separate meta-analysis of self-reported sleep. It found no significant difference in risk for sleep <7 h, which contrasts with Yin 2017. — [DOAJ/JAHA 2018](https://doaj.org/article/e01337e41c644a9ebb8999d768c88ec2)

**Cardiovascular disease and hypertension**
- Cappuccio FP, Cooper D, D'Elia L, Strazzullo P, Miller MA. "Sleep duration predicts cardiovascular outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies." *Eur Heart J* 2011;32(12):1484-1492. DOI: 10.1093/eurheartj/ehr007 — [PubMed 21300732](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21300732/)
  - Short sleep: CHD RR 1.48 (1.22-1.80), stroke 1.15 (1.00-1.31), total CVD 1.03 (0.93-1.15).
  - Long sleep: CHD 1.38 (1.15-1.66), stroke 1.65 (1.45-1.87), total CVD 1.41 (1.19-1.68).
  - Data: 15 studies, 474,684 participants, 16,067 events.
  - The authors call sleep duration "predictors, or markers" of these outcomes.
- Daghlas I, et al. "Sleep duration and myocardial infarction." *J Am Coll Cardiol* 2019;74(10):1304-1314. DOI: 10.1016/j.jacc.2019.07.022 — [PubMed 31488267](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31488267/)
  - Observational, UK Biobank (n=461,347): short sleep (<6 h) gave ~20% higher MI risk and long sleep (>9 h) ~34% higher risk, compared with 6-9 h.
  - Mendelian randomization: HR 1.19 in CARDIoGRAMplusC4D and 1.21 in UK Biobank, consistent with a causal effect.
  - People with high genetic risk for CAD who slept a healthy duration had lower MI risk (HR 0.82).
- Hosseini K, Soleimani H, Tavakoli K, et al. "Association between sleep duration and hypertension incidence: systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies." *PLoS One* 2024;19(7):e0307120. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0307120 — [PMC11249221](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11249221/)
  - Data: 16 cohorts, 1,044,035 participants, reference group 7-8 h.
  - Short sleep: <7 h HR 1.07 (1.06-1.09), <5 h HR 1.11 (1.08-1.14), 5-7 h HR 1.05 (1.00-1.10).
  - Long sleep: >8 h HR 1.01 (1.00-1.03), not significant at any level. This means the hypertension curve looks closer to L-shaped than U-shaped.
  - The association was significant in women (1.07) but not in men (1.00). The sex interaction was P<0.01.
- Wang Q, et al. "Short sleep duration is associated with hypertension risk among adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis." *Hypertens Res* 2012. DOI: 10.1038/hr.2012.91 — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/hr201291)
  - Data: 6 prospective and 17 cross-sectional studies.
  - Incident hypertension: RR 1.33 (1.11-1.61), but only in people under 65.
  - Prevalent hypertension: OR 1.20.
- Ai S, et al. *Eur Heart J* 2021;42(34):3349. UK Biobank MR of 12 CVDs (n=404,044). Genetically predicted short sleep (≤6 h) had adverse effects on 5 of the 12 CVDs, including arterial hypertension. The nonlinear MR curve for hypertension was L-shaped. — [EHJ 2021](https://dbpia.nl.go.kr/eurheartj/article/42/34/3349/6210684)

**Type 2 diabetes**
- Shan Z, et al. "Sleep duration and risk of type 2 diabetes: a meta-analysis of prospective studies." *Diabetes Care* 2015;38(3):529-537. DOI: 10.2337/dc14-2073 — [PubMed 25715415](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25715415/)
  - The relationship was U-shaped, with the lowest risk at 7-8 h.
  - RR 1.09 (1.04-1.15) per hour below 7 h and 1.14 (1.03-1.26) per hour above.
  - Data: 482,502 participants, 18,443 cases.
- Mendelian randomization for diabetes is inconsistent:
  - Wang et al. (Schooling group): sleep duration showed no association with diabetes, OR 0.85 per hour (0.64-1.13). — [PolyU](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/sleep-duration-and-risk-of-diabetes-observational-and-mendelian-r/)
  - Gao et al. 2020, *Front Genet*: insomnia, not sleep duration, showed a causal effect on T2D. — [PMC7770175](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7770175/)
  - Kuroda et al. 2025, *J Diabetes Investig*: nonlinear MR found no strong evidence. — [Fujita](https://pure.fujita-hu.ac.jp/en/publications/investigating-the-non-linear-association-between-sleep-duration-a/)

**Obesity**
- Cappuccio FP, Taggart FM, Kandala NB, et al. "Meta-analysis of short sleep duration and obesity in children and adults." *Sleep* 2008;31(5):619-626. DOI: 10.1093/sleep/31.5.619 — [PubMed 18517032](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18517032/); [PMC2398749](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2398749)
  - Children (≤10 h vs >10 h): OR 1.89 (1.46-2.43).
  - Adults (≤5 h vs >5 h): OR 1.55 (1.43-1.68).
  - Data: 36 population samples, 634,511 participants. The analysis is mainly cross-sectional, and the authors say a causal link is "unproven".

**Cancer and night shift work**
- Lu Y, et al. "Association between sleep duration and cancer risk: a meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies." *PLoS One* 2013;8(9):e74723 — [PMC3762769](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3762769/)
  - Short sleep: RR 1.05 (0.90-1.24), not significant.
  - Long sleep was associated with colorectal cancer.
- Pooled analysis of 13 cohorts (>723,000 people), *Asian Pac J Cancer Prev* 2013 — [KoreaScience PDF](https://www.koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201305981342540.pdf)
  - Short sleep: HR 1.06 (0.92-1.23). Long sleep: 0.91 (0.78-1.07).
  - No dose-response relationship was found.
- Chen et al. 2018, a separate meta-analysis. It reported short sleep associated with cancer only among Asians (OR 1.36, 1.02-1.80), with no dose-response relationship. — [PubMed 24460326 / related](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24460326/)
- IARC Monographs Working Group. "Carcinogenicity of night shift work." *Lancet Oncol* 2019;20(8):1058-1059. DOI: 10.1016/S1470-2045(19)30455-3. Full volume: IARC Monographs Vol. 124 (2020). — [IARC Vol 124](https://www.iarc.who.int/news-events/iarc-monographs-volume-124-night-shift-work); [IARC Q&A](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/QA_Monographs_Volume124.pdf); [ESMO](https://esmo.org/Oncology-News/Night-Shift-Work-Classified-as-Probably-Carcinogenic-to-Humans)
  - Classification: Group 2A, "probably carcinogenic".
  - Evidence: limited in humans (breast, prostate, colon and rectum), sufficient in animals, and strong mechanistic evidence (immunosuppression, inflammation, cell proliferation).
  - History: Volume 98 (2010) already classified it as 2A, under the name "shift work that involves circadian disruption".
  - The classification describes the strength of evidence for a hazard, not the size of the risk.
  - Note: this concerns circadian disruption, not sleep duration as such.

**Dementia and Alzheimer's disease**
- Sabia S, Fayosse A, Dumurgier J, et al. "Association of sleep duration in middle and old age with incidence of dementia." *Nat Commun* 2021;12:2289. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-021-22354-2 — [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10126499/1/Sabia%20-%20Sleep%20and%20dementia%20risk.pdf); [UCL news](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2021/apr/lack-sleep-middle-age-linked-dementia-risk)
  - Hazard ratios for ≤6 h vs 7 h: 1.22 at age 50, 1.37 at age 60, and 1.24 (0.98-1.57) at age 70.
  - The widely reported "~30% higher risk" refers to persistent short sleep at ages 50, 60 and 70. That figure was reported in the press and recalled from the abstract; I did not re-read it verbatim.
  - Sleep was self-reported six times between 1985 and 2016, and a subset was checked with accelerometers.
  - The authors cannot establish causality. They raise prodromal disease as a possible explanation for the weaker effect at age 70.
- Shokri-Kojori E, Wang GJ, Wiers CE, et al. "β-Amyloid accumulation in the human brain after one night of sleep deprivation." *PNAS* 2018;115(17):4483-4488. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1721694115 — [NIH news](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/lack-sleep-may-be-linked-risk-factor-alzheimers-disease)
  - Design: n=20, aged 22-72, PET with 18F-florbetaben, after about 31 h awake.
  - Result: Aβ rose ~5% in the right hippocampus and thalamus. Larger increases went with worse mood. The rise was unrelated to APOE genotype.
  - Fewer self-reported sleep hours were associated with higher baseline Aβ.
  - Caveats: small sample, and it is unknown whether the rise reverses after recovery sleep. The relationship is bidirectional.
- Xie L, Kang H, Xu Q, et al. (Nedergaard). "Sleep drives metabolite clearance from the adult brain." *Science* 2013;342:373-377. DOI: 10.1126/science.1241224 — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/10/131017144636.htm); [PDF](https://www.polyu.edu.hk/cbs/rclcn/images/cdl_articles/X/Xie_et_al._2013.pdf)
  - In mice, sleep expanded the interstitial space by ~60% and increased clearance of β-amyloid.
  - A companion review reports clearance during sleep up to about 2x faster than during waking. — [PubMed 24199995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24199995/)

**Depression, anxiety and suicide**
- Zhai L, Zhang H, Zhang D. "Sleep duration and depression among adults: a meta-analysis of prospective studies." *Depress Anxiety* 2015;32(9):664-670. DOI: 10.1002/da.22386 — [PubMed 26047492](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26047492)
  - Short sleep: RR 1.31 (1.04-1.64). Long sleep: RR 1.42 (1.04-1.92).
  - Data: 7 prospective studies, I²=0%, no publication bias.
- A 2023 dose-response meta-analysis in middle-aged and older adults found a U-shape with the nadir at ~7 h: RR 1.09 at 5 h and 1.31 at 9 h. — [Front Physiol 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1085091/full)
- Suicide:
  - An adolescent dose-response meta-analysis (12 studies, ~598,000 people) found ideation and attempts lowest at 8-9 h. Suicide plans fell 11% for each extra hour of sleep. — [TMU hub](https://hub.tmu.edu.tw/en/publications/associations-between-sleep-duration-and-suicidality-in-adolescent/)
  - In a Korean nationwide survey, ≤5 h gave aOR 1.46 for ideation and 2.05 for attempts. — [PMC8197181](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8197181/)
  - NESDA (Netherlands), adults with depression or anxiety: ≤6 h gave aOR 1.68 for a past suicide attempt. — [Psychol Med](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A33615CE83C2ED89C0178EFA462224C7/S0033291720000860a.pdf/div-class-title-suicidal-ideation-and-suicide-attempts-associations-with-sleep-duration-insomnia-and-inflammation-div.pdf)
  - This evidence is mostly cross-sectional.
- Perinatal depression (MR): nonlinear MR found that genetically predicted shorter sleep increased perinatal depression. — [BMC Med 2022, PMC9465870](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9465870/)

**Accidents (drowsy driving)**
- Tefft BC. "Acute sleep deprivation and risk of motor vehicle crash involvement." AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety, December 2016 — [AAA Foundation](https://aaafoundation.org/acute-sleep-deprivation-risk-motor-vehicle-crash-involvement/); [AASM](https://aasm.org/aaa-study-links-sleep-loss-to-increased-motor-vehicle-crash-risk/)
  - Crash risk compared with ≥7 h of sleep: 6-7 h gives 1.3x, 5-6 h 1.9x, 4-5 h 4.3x, and <4 h 11.5x.
  - The 4-5 h risk is comparable to driving at a BAC of about 0.08 (NHTSA estimates).
  - Data: 7,234 drivers in 4,571 crashes.
  - Risk may be underestimated because data for crashes between midnight and 6 a.m. were unavailable.

**Economic cost**
- Hafner M, Stepanek M, Taylor J, Troxel WM, van Stolk C. *Why Sleep Matters — The Economic Costs of Insufficient Sleep.* RAND Corporation, RR-1791, 2016 — [RAND press](https://www.rand.org/news/press/2016/11/30.html); [RAND commentary](https://www.rand.org/pubs/commentary/2016/11/americans-dont-sleep-enough-and-its-costing-us.html)
  - USA: up to $411 bn/yr (2.28% of GDP) and ~1.2 M lost workdays/yr.
  - Other countries: Japan 2.92% of GDP, UK 1.86%. Canada and Germany were also modeled.
  - Model input: <6 h sleep carries 13% higher mortality than 7-9 h.
  - Moving people from <6 h to 6-7 h would add $226.4 bn/yr to the US economy.
  - These are modeled estimates, not measured totals.

**Recommendations and prevalence**
- Watson NF, Badr MS, Belenky G, et al. "Recommended amount of sleep for a healthy adult: a joint consensus statement of the AASM and SRS." Published simultaneously in *Sleep* 2015;38(6):843-844 and *J Clin Sleep Med* 2015;11(6):591-592, so both citations are valid. — [AASM PDF](https://www.aasm.org/resources/pdf/adultsleepdurationconsensus.pdf)
  - Recommendation: adults should sleep ≥7 h per night regularly, decided with the modified RAND Appropriateness Method.
  - More than 9 h may be appropriate for young adults, people recovering from sleep debt, or people who are ill.
- CDC (Liu Y, et al. *MMWR* 2016;65:137-141): 35% of US adults reported <7 h of sleep (BRFSS 2014). — [AASM summary](https://aasm.org/cdc-publishes-new-estimates-of-u-s-adult-sleep-duration/)

### Inferences
- Short sleep is associated with about 10-50% higher relative risk across the major chronic diseases. These are modest effects for an individual. Because ~35% of adults sleep less than 7 h, the population-attributable burden could still be large; that last point is my inference.
- The CHD association (RR 1.48) is stronger than the association for stroke or total CVD. The Daghlas MR result supports MI specifically.
- Short sleep does not raise overall cancer risk. The cancer concern applies to circadian disruption from night work, which is a different exposure.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve confidence intervals for the Sabia 2021 hazard ratios at ages 50 and 60. The persistent-short-sleep HR of ~1.30 is from recall and press coverage, not a verbatim re-read of the abstract.
- I found no recent large meta-analysis specifically on short sleep and **anxiety** disorders.
- Obesity in children: I did not retrieve newer longitudinal meta-analyses (for example Fatima 2015, or Miller 2018 in *Sleep*).
- Hypertension: I did not get the pooled estimates from Meng 2013 (prospective cohorts).
- RAND's international dollar figures for countries other than the USA were not retrieved.

## How strong is the causal evidence (Mendelian randomization, experimental support)?

### Takeaway
The causal evidence is moderate for cardiovascular outcomes and weak to mixed for metabolic and neurodegenerative ones.
- **MI and hypertension:** MR studies support short sleep as a cause of MI (Daghlas 2019) and of hypertension and some other CVDs (Ai 2021).
- **Type 2 diabetes:** MR is null or inconsistent for sleep duration; insomnia looks more likely to be the causal trait.
- **Dementia:** cohort data are vulnerable to reverse causation from preclinical disease. Mechanistic studies (glymphatic clearance in mice, ~5% Aβ rise after one night in humans) are plausible but small.
- **Mortality and cancer:** these remain associational.

### Cited findings
- Mendelian randomization for MI: genetically predicted short sleep gave HR 1.19 (CARDIoGRAMplusC4D) and 1.21 (UK Biobank). The authors call short sleep "a potentially causal risk factor" and suggest testing sleep extension. — [PubMed 31488267](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31488267/); [ACC](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Journal-Scans/2019/09/04/14/45/Sleep-Duration-and-Myocardial-Infarction)
- Mendelian randomization for CVD: genetically predicted short sleep (≤6 h) had adverse effects on 5 of 12 CVDs, including hypertension, with an L-shaped nonlinear curve. — [Ai 2021 EHJ](https://dbpia.nl.go.kr/eurheartj/article/42/34/3349/6210684)
- Mendelian randomization for diabetes: null for sleep duration (OR 0.85 per hour, 0.64-1.13). Insomnia shows a causal signal instead. — [PolyU](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/sleep-duration-and-risk-of-diabetes-observational-and-mendelian-r/); [Gao 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7770175/)
- Mendelian randomization for perinatal depression: nonlinear effects found; shorter sleep raised perinatal depression risk. — [PMC9465870](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9465870/)
- Experimental, Aβ: one night of total sleep deprivation raised Aβ by ~5% (n=20). The authors call for larger studies and note the relationship is bidirectional. — [NIH](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/nih-research-matters/sleep-deprivation-increases-alzheimers-protein)
- Mechanism in mice: sleep expands the interstitial space by 60% and speeds Aβ clearance. — [Xie 2013](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/10/131017144636.htm)
- The original authors themselves describe their results as associations:
  - Cappuccio 2011 calls sleep duration "predictors, or markers". — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21300732/)
  - Cappuccio 2008 calls the obesity link "unproven" as causal. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18517032/)
  - Sabia 2021 says causality cannot be established and raises prodromal disease. — [UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/population-health-sciences/news/2021/apr/lack-sleep-middle-age-linked-dementia-risk)
- Self-report bias: most cohorts measure sleep by questionnaire.
  - Cappuccio 2010 used questionnaires only. — [PMC2864873](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864873/)
  - Sabia 2021 checked self-reports with accelerometry in a subset. — [Futura](https://www.futura-sciences.com/en/how-many-hours-should-you-sleep-to-rest-your-brain_31127/)
- Definitions are heterogeneous: "short" ranges from <4 h to ≤6 h across studies. — [stafforini](https://stafforini.com/works/cappuccio-2010-sleep-duration-allcause/)
- Results conflict for mortality: Kwok 2018 (*JAHA*) found no significant mortality increase for <7 h, whereas Cappuccio 2010 and Yin 2017 did. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/e01337e41c644a9ebb8999d768c88ec2)

### Inferences
- **Reverse causation:** depression, pain, illness and preclinical dementia can shorten sleep, and they can lengthen it. This is likely to inflate observational estimates. It probably explains much of the long-sleep risk as well.
- **Misclassification:** self-reported time in bed typically differs from actual sleep. This likely biases toward the null, or distorts which categories carry the risk.
- **Confounding:** residual confounding (socioeconomic status, shift work, sleep apnea, BMI) probably remains, despite adjustment.
- **Hierarchy of causal evidence** (my synthesis):
  - Strongest: drowsy-driving crashes, where the acute mechanism is clear.
  - Next: CHD/MI and hypertension, supported by MR.
  - Weaker: obesity, T2D, depression and dementia, where experimental short-term data are plausible but MR is mixed or missing.
  - Weakest: overall cancer, with no association.

### Gaps
- I found no MR study of sleep duration and dementia/Alzheimer's or all-cause mortality in this session. Such studies exist in the literature but were not verified here.
- I found no MR on general adult depression or anxiety in this session.
- Long-term RCTs of sleep extension with hard clinical endpoints appear not to exist; this was not confirmed by a search.

## Is there a U-shape and what's the optimal range?

### Takeaway
Yes, for all-cause mortality, CVD/stroke, T2D and depression. The nadir is about 7 h (Yin 2017), or 7-8 h (Shan 2015). Long sleep (≥9 h) often shows equal or larger relative risks than short sleep (mortality RR 1.30 vs 1.12), most likely reflecting confounding by illness. For hypertension the curve is closer to L-shaped: short sleep raises risk, and long sleep is not significant. The consensus recommendation for adults is at least 7 h per night (AASM/SRS 2015). For adolescent suicidality the nadir is 8-9 h.

### Cited findings
- Mortality, long vs short sleep: RR 1.30 vs 1.12. — [Cappuccio 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864873/)
- U-shape with the lowest risk at ≈7 h: for each hour above 7 h, mortality rises 13%; for each hour below, 6%. — [Yin 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634263)
- Long sleep has larger relative risks than short sleep for stroke (1.65 vs 1.15) and total CVD (1.41 vs 1.03). — [Cappuccio 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21300732/)
- T2D: U-shape with the nadir at 7-8 h. — [Shan 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25715415/)
- Hypertension: long sleep is not significant, so the curve is closer to L-shaped. — [Hosseini 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11249221/); [Ai 2021 MR](https://dbpia.nl.go.kr/eurheartj/article/42/34/3349/6210684)
- Depression: long sleep 1.42 vs short sleep 1.31. Older adults have a nadir at ~7 h. — [Zhai 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26047492); [Front Physiol 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1085091/full)
- Dementia: the reference was 7 h. Short sleep in midlife was associated with risk; the long-sleep association in Whitehall was not highlighted in the sources retrieved. — [Sabia 2021](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10126499/1/Sabia%20-%20Sleep%20and%20dementia%20risk.pdf)
- MI: both <6 h (~+20%) and >9 h (~+34%) were associated with higher risk than 6-9 h. — [Daghlas 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31488267/)
- Recommendation: adults should sleep ≥7 h, with no upper limit set; >9 h may be appropriate in some groups. — [AASM/SRS 2015](https://www.aasm.org/resources/pdf/adultsleepdurationconsensus.pdf)
- Adolescent suicidality is lowest at 8-9 h. — [TMU hub](https://hub.tmu.edu.tw/en/publications/associations-between-sleep-duration-and-suicidality-in-adolescent/)

### Inferences
- The long-sleep part of the U is probably mostly confounding or reverse causation: underlying disease, depression, low activity, and the measurement of time in bed rather than sleep. The short-sleep part has more mechanistic and MR support, at least for CVD and hypertension. This is consistent with the AASM/SRS choice to set a minimum (≥7 h) but no maximum.
- The practical optimal range for adults is 7-8 h, and 7-9 h is acceptable.

### Gaps
- Individual sleep need varies, and few studies use objective, multi-night measurement to define the true optimum.
- Optimal ranges for children are not covered by these sources beyond the Cappuccio 2008 cutoff (10 h). The AASM pediatric consensus (Paruthi 2016) was not retrieved.

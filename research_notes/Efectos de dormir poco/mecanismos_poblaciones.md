## Q1. Why does sleep loss cause each main effect? (the chain from cause to effect)

### Takeaway
Two processes control sleep. Adenosine-linked sleep pressure builds the longer you are awake, and the circadian clock sets the timing. Each sleep stage does specific work: slow-wave sleep (SWS) drives growth-hormone release, synaptic downscaling, memory transfer from the hippocampus to the cortex, and possibly brain waste clearance. REM sleep handles emotional processing. Losing sleep removes these processes and keeps the stress systems switched on. In practice that means:
- the prefrontal cortex loses control over the amygdala;
- the hippocampus encodes new memories less well;
- sympathetic activity and evening cortisol rise;
- insulin sensitivity falls and appetite hormones shift toward hunger;
- low-grade inflammation increases.

Much of the human evidence comes from small laboratory studies (n≈10–30). The glymphatic "brain washing" idea rests mostly on mouse data and is now disputed.

### Cited Findings

**1a. How sleep is regulated: the two-process model**
- Borbély's two-process model: Process S is homeostatic sleep pressure. It rises during waking and falls during sleep, and it is indexed by slow-wave (delta) activity in the NREM EEG. Process C is the circadian rhythm generated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus. Their interaction sets when we sleep and for how long. — Borbély AA (1982) "A two process model of sleep regulation." *Human Neurobiology* 1:195–204 (no DOI); reappraisal: Borbély AA, Daan S, Wirz-Justice A, Deboer T (2016) *J Sleep Res* 25:131–143, https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.12371 **[K]**
- Adenosine as a chemical sign of sleep pressure: extracellular adenosine in the basal forebrain rose during prolonged waking and fell during recovery sleep. This was shown in **cats** **[ANIMAL]**. — Porkka-Heiskanen T et al. (1997) "Adenosine: a mediator of the sleep-inducing effects of prolonged wakefulness." *Science* 276:1265–1268, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.276.5316.1265 **[K]**. Caffeine works by blocking adenosine A1/A2A receptors. That is why it hides sleep pressure without reducing it (mechanistic inference, standard pharmacology).
- Lost sleep behaves like extra wakefulness that carries a building neurobiological cost. In Van Dongen 2003, wakefulness beyond about 15.84 h per day predicted performance lapses. — Van Dongen HPA, Maislin G, Mullington JM, Dinges DF (2003) "The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness." *Sleep* 26(2):117–126, https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/) **[V]**

**1b. Sleep stages and what each does**
- SWS and growth hormone: in men, about 70% or more of daily GH secretion happens during sleep, mostly in SWS. Between ages 16 and 25 and 36 and 50, SWS fell from about 19% to about 3% of sleep, and GH secretion fell in parallel. — Van Cauter E, Leproult R, Plat L (2000) "Age-related changes in slow wave sleep and REM sleep and relationship with growth hormone and cortisol levels in healthy men." *JAMA* 284(7):861–868, https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.284.7.861 **[K]**
- SWS and synaptic homeostasis: the synaptic homeostasis hypothesis holds that waking strengthens synapses overall and SWS scales them back down. This restores learning capacity and saves energy. Much of the direct evidence is from **rodents and flies** **[ANIMAL, largely]**. — Tononi G, Cirelli C (2014) "Sleep and the price of plasticity." *Neuron* 81(1):12–34, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.12.025 **[K]**
- SWS and memory consolidation: hippocampal sharp-wave ripples, thalamocortical spindles and slow oscillations work together to move memories from the hippocampus to the neocortex. — Reviewed in Krause AJ, Simon EB, Mander BA, Greer SM, Saletin JM, Goldstein-Piekarski AN, Walker MP (2017) "The sleep-deprived human brain." *Nat Rev Neurosci* 18:404–418, https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn.2017.55 **[K]**
- SWS and glymphatic clearance **[ANIMAL]**: in **mice**, natural sleep and anesthesia expanded the interstitial space by about 60%. This increased exchange between cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and interstitial fluid and sped up β-amyloid clearance, which reviews describe as up to roughly 2× faster than during waking. — Xie L, Kang H, Xu Q, Chen MJ, Liao Y, Thiyagarajan M, O'Donnell J, Christensen DJ, Nicholson C, Iliff JJ, Takano T, Deane R, Nedergaard M (2013) "Sleep drives metabolite clearance from the adult brain." *Science* 342(6156):373–377, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1241224 — [PMC3880190 / search record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3867735) **[V]**
- **Contradicting evidence (2024)** **[ANIMAL]**: using direct fluorescent-dye tracking in mice, Miao et al. found brain clearance was about 30% *lower* in sleep and about 50% lower under anesthesia than in waking. Plog and Kipnis criticised the method, including the injection volume. Verkman supported the result. The question is not settled. — Miao A, Luo T, Hsieh B, Edge CJ, Gridley M, Wong RTC, Constandinou TG, Wisden W, Franks NP (2024) "Brain clearance is reduced during sleep and anesthesia." *Nat Neurosci* 27(6):1046–1050, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-024-01638-y — [Alzforum coverage](https://alzforum.org/news/research-news/does-sleep-really-speed-clearance-brain); [Imperial College news](https://www.imperial.ac.uk/news/253273/scientists-find-sleep-clear-brain-toxins/) **[V]** (DOI from prior knowledge)
- Human data that fits a clearance role: in human NREM sleep, large slow pulses of CSF move in step with slow neural waves. — Fultz NE et al. (2019) *Science* 366:628–631, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aax5440 **[K]**. After one night without sleep, β-amyloid on PET rose by about 5% in the hippocampus and thalamus. — Shokri-Kojori E et al. (2018) *PNAS* 115(17):4483–4488, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1721694115 **[K]**. Neither study shows that *glymphatic* flow specifically is the mechanism.
- REM sleep and emotional processing: under the "sleep to forget, sleep to remember" model, REM sleep, with its low noradrenaline and active limbic regions, keeps the content of emotional memories while weakening their emotional charge. — Walker MP, van der Helm E (2009) "Overnight therapy? The role of sleep in emotional brain processing." *Psychol Bull* 135(5):731–748, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016570 **[K]**

**1c. Mechanisms by organ system**

*Brain: prefrontal cortex and amygdala*
- After about 35 h awake, amygdala responses to negative images rose sharply and the amygdala's functional connection to the medial prefrontal cortex weakened. This points to a loss of top-down emotional control. The study used fMRI with n≈26. — Yoo SS, Gujar N, Hu PT, Jolesz FA, Walker MP (2007) "The human emotional brain without sleep — a prefrontal amygdala disconnect." *Curr Biol* 17(20):R877–R878, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2007.08.007 — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/10/071022124729.htm) **[V]**. *Caveat:* the widely quoted "~60% greater amygdala reactivity" figure could not be traced to the paper text this session.
- Attention and vigilance decline the most, and complex executive functions decline somewhat less. — Lim J, Dinges DF (2010) "A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables." *Psychol Bull* 136(3):375–389, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018883 **[K]**

*Brain: hippocampus and memory*
- One night of sleep deprivation cut the ability to encode new memories by about 40% and lowered hippocampal activation during encoding. — Yoo SS, Hu PT, Gujar N, Jolesz FA, Walker MP (2007) "A deficit in the ability to form new human memories without sleep." *Nat Neurosci* 10:385–392, https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1851 **[K]**
- Chronic restriction: 14 days at 6 h per night produced growing, dose-dependent deficits on every task. Subjective sleepiness levelled off, so people underestimated how impaired they were. — Van Dongen et al. 2003 (above) **[V]**

*Cardiovascular*
- Proposed chain: short sleep leads to higher sympathetic tone and lower vagal tone. That raises nighttime blood pressure and stops the normal night-time "dipping". Together with inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, this leads to hypertension, coronary disease and stroke. In the lab, one night of total sleep deprivation raised muscle sympathetic nerve activity and blood pressure in healthy humans. — Dettoni JL et al. (2012) "Cardiovascular effects of partial sleep deprivation in healthy volunteers." *J Appl Physiol* 113(2):232–236, https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.01604.2011 **[K]**. Sleep restriction also increased the ratio of sympathetic to vagal activity on heart-rate variability. — Spiegel et al. 1999 (below) **[K]**
- In a meta-analysis of prospective studies, short sleep was associated with higher coronary heart disease risk (RR ≈1.48) and stroke risk (RR ≈1.15). — Cappuccio FP, Cooper D, D'Elia L, Strazzullo P, Miller MA (2011) *Eur Heart J* 32(12):1484–1492, https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehr007 **[K]**
- Mortality: short sleep RR ≈1.12. Long sleep is also associated with higher mortality, a U-shaped curve that probably partly reflects illness causing long sleep. — Cappuccio FP et al. (2010) *Sleep* 33(5):585–592, https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/33.5.585 **[K]**

*Metabolic: insulin, appetite hormones, cortisol*
- After 6 nights of 4 h in bed, glucose tolerance fell, evening cortisol rose, and sympathetic activity rose, compared with 12 h in bed. — Spiegel K, Leproult R, Van Cauter E (1999) "Impact of sleep debt on metabolic and endocrine function." *Lancet* 354(9188):1435–1439, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(99)01376-8 **[K]**
- After 2 nights of 4 h, leptin fell by 18%, ghrelin rose by 28%, hunger rose by 24% and appetite by 23%. The appetite increase was strongest for calorie-dense, carbohydrate-rich foods. The study was a randomized crossover in 12 young men. — Spiegel K, Tasali E, Penev P, Van Cauter E (2004) "Brief communication: Sleep curtailment in healthy young men is associated with decreased leptin levels, elevated ghrelin levels, and increased hunger and appetite." *Ann Intern Med* 141(11):846–850, https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-141-11-200412070-00008 — [PubMed 15583226](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15583226/) **[V]**
- Cortisol: partial or total sleep loss raised cortisol the following evening, which delays the normal evening fall. Higher evening cortisol is linked to insulin resistance and memory impairment. — Leproult R, Copinschi G, Buxton O, Van Cauter E (1997) "Sleep loss results in an elevation of cortisol levels the next evening." *Sleep* 20(10):865–870, https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865 **[K]**
- During a calorie-restricted diet, sleeping 5.5 h instead of 8.5 h cut the share of weight lost as fat by about 55% and increased the loss of lean mass. — Nedeltcheva AV et al. (2010) *Ann Intern Med* 153(7):435–441, https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-153-7-201010050-00006 **[K]**

*Immune and inflammation*
- A meta-analysis of 72 studies with more than 50,000 participants found that sleep disturbance (poor quality or insomnia) was associated with higher CRP and IL-6, and long sleep (>8 h) was associated with higher CRP and IL-6. TNF-α showed no association. **Conflict over short sleep:** a press summary says short sleep was associated with higher CRP, while a secondary summary quotes the abstract as finding an association "but not [with] short sleep duration." Experimental sleep deprivation reportedly did not consistently raise these markers. Check the original abstract. — Irwin MR, Olmstead R, Carroll JE (2016) "Sleep disturbance, sleep duration, and inflammation: a systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies and experimental sleep deprivation." *Biol Psychiatry* 80(1):40–52, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2015.05.014 — [UCLA](https://teams.semel.ucla.edu/node/3739); [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/releases/311494.php) **[V]** (DOI from prior knowledge)
- Infection susceptibility: in 164 adults deliberately given rhinovirus, those who slept less than 6 h (actigraphy) had **4.2 times** the risk of a clinical cold compared with those who slept more than 7 h. At 5 h or less the risk was 4.5 times. — Prather AA, Janicki-Deverts D, Hall MH, Cohen S (2015) "Behaviorally assessed sleep and susceptibility to the common cold." *Sleep* 38(9):1353–1359, https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4968 — [UCSF](https://www.ucsf.edu/news/2015/08/131411/short-sleepers-are-four-times-more-likely-catch-cold) **[V]**
- Vaccine response: sleep restriction around influenza vaccination roughly halved antibody titres at 10 days. — Spiegel K, Sheridan JF, Van Cauter E (2002) *JAMA* 288(12):1471–1472, https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.288.12.1471 **[K]**
- How sleep supports immunity: sleep moves T cells to lymph nodes, raises growth hormone, prolactin and pro-inflammatory signalling that support adaptive immunity, and lowers cortisol and catecholamines. Chronic short sleep turns this into constant low-grade inflammation driven by NF-κB. — Besedovsky L, Lange T, Haack M (2019) "The sleep-immune crosstalk in health and disease." *Physiol Rev* 99(3):1325–1380, https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00010.2018 **[K]**

*Skin*
- Chronically poor sleepers showed more signs of intrinsic skin ageing, slower recovery of the skin barrier after tape stripping, and slower recovery from UV redness. The study was small, in women, and funded by industry (Estée Lauder). — Oyetakin-White P et al. (2015) "Does poor sleep quality affect skin ageing?" *Clin Exp Dermatol* 40(1):17–22, https://doi.org/10.1111/ced.12455 **[K]**
- Observers rated photos of sleep-deprived faces as less healthy and more tired. — Axelsson J et al. (2010) "Beauty sleep." *BMJ* 341:c6614, https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.c6614 **[K]**
- Proposed mechanism: higher cortisol and inflammation, plus less nighttime GH-driven tissue repair. This is inferred and not directly shown in skin.

*Hormones and reproduction*
- Sleeping less than 5 h for about 8 nights lowered daytime testosterone by **10–15%** in 10 healthy young men averaging 24 years old. The press release compares this to 10–15 years of ageing. — Leproult R, Van Cauter E (2011) "Effect of 1 week of sleep restriction on testosterone levels in young healthy men." *JAMA* 305(21):2173–2174, https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2011.710 — [UChicago](https://www.uchicagomedicine.org/forefront/news/2011/may/sleep-loss-lowers-testosterone-in-healthy-young-men); [PMC4445839](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/4445839/) **[V]** (DOI from prior knowledge)
- Female reproduction: no high-quality mechanistic source was found this session (see Gaps).

*Gut microbiome*
- After 2 nights of partial sleep deprivation in 9 normal-weight men, the Firmicutes:Bacteroidetes ratio rose, as did Coriobacteriaceae and Erysipelotrichaceae, while Tenericutes fell. Beta diversity and short-chain fatty acids did not change. Insulin sensitivity fell. The authors concluded that the microbiome changes **may not be the central mechanism** behind the short-term drop in insulin sensitivity. — Benedict C et al. (2016) "Gut microbiota and glucometabolic alterations in response to recurrent partial sleep deprivation in normal-weight young individuals." *Mol Metab* 5(12):1175–1186, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molmet.2016.10.003 — [PMC5123208](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5123208/) **[V]**
- Much of the evidence on circadian disruption and the microbiome (e.g., jet-lag models) comes from **mice** **[ANIMAL]** (general knowledge; not sourced this session).

### Inferences
- Several organ effects seem to share a few upstream drivers: high sympathetic tone, a shifted cortisol rhythm, and inflammation. This would explain why cardiovascular, metabolic and immune effects tend to appear together.
- The model "you misjudge your own impairment because subjective sleepiness levels off" (Van Dongen 2003) also fits the Drake 2013 caffeine finding, where people did not notice they had lost an hour of sleep. Self-report is a poor guide to sleep debt.
- Glymphatic clearance should be presented as an *attractive hypothesis supported by mouse data and disputed by newer mouse data*. It is not an established human mechanism.

### Gaps
- The full abstract of Irwin 2016 could not be fetched (PubMed blocked), so whether short sleep is associated with CRP remains unresolved. Check it.
- No human mechanistic source on female reproductive hormones (menstrual cycle, fertility) was retrieved.
- The "~60% amygdala" figure from Yoo 2007 was not verified in the primary text.
- Human sympathetic and skin studies are small. Long-term causal data in humans (rather than cohort associations) are lacking for heart disease and the microbiome.

## Q2. Who is most vulnerable?

### Takeaway
Vulnerability is a stable individual trait (some people are resilient, others are not). Adolescents are especially at risk because their circadian timing shifts later while school starts early. Shift workers carry real disease risk. "Natural short sleepers" exist but are extremely rare carriers of specific mutations (DEC2/BHLHE41, ADRB1, among others), so they do not justify sleeping less in general. Older adults need about the same amount of sleep but produce less SWS.

### Cited Findings
- **Trait vulnerability:** under repeated total sleep deprivation, individual differences in performance impairment were stable and trait-like, with high intraclass correlations, and were largely separate from subjective sleepiness. — Van Dongen HPA, Baynard MD, Maislin G, Dinges DF (2004) "Systematic interindividual differences in neurobehavioral impairment from sleep loss: evidence of trait-like differential vulnerability." *Sleep* 27(3):423–433, https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/27.3.423 **[K]**
- **DEC2/BHLHE41 short sleepers:** a mother and daughter carrying the heterozygous DEC2 **P385R** mutation slept about 6.25 h per night versus about 8.06 h in non-carrier relatives. Transgenic **mice** with the mutation also slept less **[ANIMAL confirmation]**. The proposed mechanism is weaker repression of CLOCK/BMAL1, with possible effects on orexin. — He Y, Jones CR, Fujiki N, Xu Y, Guo B, Holder JL Jr, Rossner MJ, Nishino S, Fu YH (2009) "The transcriptional repressor DEC2 regulates sleep length in mammals." *Science* 325(5942):866–870, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1174443 — [UCSF news](https://www.ucsf.edu/news/2009/08/4281/first-human-gene-implicated-regulating-length-human-sleep) **[V]** (DOI from prior knowledge)
- **ADRB1 short sleepers (FNSS2):** a rare heterozygous A187V missense variant in the β1-adrenergic receptor was found in one family (kindred 50025). Carriers slept about 5.7 h versus about 7.9 h. The variant receptor was less stable and produced less cAMP in cells. Knock-in **mice** slept less, and ADRB1+ neurons in the dorsal pons promoted wakefulness **[ANIMAL]**. Only one family has been reported. — Shi G, Xing L, Wu D, Bhattacharyya BJ, Jones CR, McMahon T, Chong SYC, Chen JA, Coppola G, Geschwind D, Krystal A, Ptáček LJ, Fu YH (2019) "A rare mutation of β1-adrenergic receptor affects sleep/wake behaviors." *Neuron* 103(6):1044–1055.e7, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.07.026 — [PubMed 31473062](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31473062/); [PMC6763376](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6763376) **[V]**
- **Adolescents:** AASM recommends **8–10 h per 24 h** for ages 13–18. The recommendation was reached by a 13-member panel using a modified RAND method that reviewed 864 articles, and it was endorsed by the AAP. — Paruthi S et al. (2016) "Recommended amount of sleep for pediatric populations: a consensus statement of the AASM." *J Clin Sleep Med* 12(6):785–786, https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.5866; methods paper *JCSM* 12(11):1549–1561, https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.6288 — [AASM PDF](https://aasm.org/resources/pdf/pediatricsleepdurationmethods.pdf) **[V]** (DOIs from prior knowledge). The mechanism behind adolescent vulnerability is a puberty-related delay in melatonin timing and slower build-up of sleep pressure, colliding with early school start times (Carskadon lab literature; not retrieved this session).
- **Older adults:** NREM slow waves decline with age, partly because the medial prefrontal cortex shrinks. This impairs overnight memory consolidation and may link poor sleep with amyloid build-up. Older adults' need for sleep does not clearly fall; their *ability* to produce deep sleep falls. — Mander BA, Winer JR, Walker MP (2017) "Sleep and human aging." *Neuron* 94(1):19–36, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2017.02.004 **[K]**; age-related SWS decline also in Van Cauter 2000 (above) **[K]**
- **Shift workers:** IARC classified night shift work as "probably carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2A) in 2019 (Monograph vol. 124). — IARC Monographs Vol 124 Group (2019) *Lancet Oncol* 20(8):1058–1059, https://doi.org/10.1016/S1470-2045(19)30455-3 **[K]**. The proposed mechanism is circadian misalignment. Sleep pressure and the clock signal fall out of step, so workers eat and are active during their biological night, which suppresses melatonin and disrupts glucose metabolism.
- **Pilots and long-haul crews** (an occupational group): see the NASA cockpit-nap study under Q3.

### Inferences
- "I'm fine on 5–6 h" is far more often a sign of poor insight (sleepiness levels off while performance keeps declining, per Van Dongen 2003) than of a short-sleeper genotype, which has been found in only a handful of families.
- The most vulnerable groups combine a heavy need for sleep or misaligned timing with external constraints: teenagers with early school starts, night-shift workers, and people with high trait vulnerability in safety-critical jobs.

### Gaps
- No population prevalence figure for familial natural short sleep could be verified. It is described as very rare, with only a few families reported.
- Vulnerability by sex, and specific data for pregnancy and menopause, were not researched this session.
- No specific citation was retrieved for adolescent circadian delay (e.g., Carskadon 2011 or Crowley 2018).

## Q3. What actually reduces or reverses the harm, and what doesn't?

### Takeaway
The only thing that fully fixes sleep loss is enough regular sleep. Short naps of about 10–30 min and caffeine within limits temporarily ease *sleepiness and performance* problems, but they do not cure the underlying deficit. One or two recovery nights after a chronic deficit do not fully restore performance. A weekend of catch-up sleep did **not** prevent metabolic harm. Extending sleep improves performance in athletes. For chronic insomnia, CBT-I is the first-line treatment with a strong recommendation, while sleep hygiene *on its own* is recommended against as a treatment.

### Cited Findings

**Recommended durations by age (for reference)**
- NSF 2015 recommended ranges: newborns (0–3 mo) 14–17 h; infants (4–11 mo) 12–15 h; toddlers (1–2 y) 11–14 h; preschool (3–5 y) 10–13 h; school-age (6–13 y) 9–11 h; teenagers (14–17 y) 8–10 h; young adults (18–25 y) and adults (26–64 y) 7–9 h; older adults (≥65 y) 7–8 h. The panel used the RAND/UCLA appropriateness method, and the ranges apply to healthy people. — Hirshkowitz M, Whiton K, Albert SM, et al. (2015) "National Sleep Foundation's sleep time duration recommendations: methodology and results summary." *Sleep Health* 1(1):40–43, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2014.12.010; final report: *Sleep Health* 1(4):233–243, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2015.10.004 — [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/national-sleep-foundations-sleep-time-duration-recommendations-me/) **[V]** (DOIs from prior knowledge)
- AASM/SRS adults: **≥7 h per night regularly** for ages 18–60. — Watson NF et al. (2015) "Recommended amount of sleep for a healthy adult: a joint consensus statement of the AASM and SRS." *Sleep* 38(6):843–844, https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4716 **[K]**
- AASM pediatric: 4–12 mo 12–16 h; 1–2 y 11–14 h; 3–5 y 10–13 h; 6–12 y 9–12 h; 13–18 y 8–10 h (naps included for the younger groups). — Paruthi 2016 (above) **[V]**

**Naps**
- In the NASA/FAA study of long-haul crews, a planned 40-minute rest opportunity in the cockpit seat produced **25.8 min** of sleep on average. Pilots fell asleep in 5.6 min and slept on 93% of opportunities. The nap was "associated with improved physiological alertness and performance compared to the No-Rest Group," with benefits seen during descent and landing. — Rosekind MR, Graeber RC, Dinges DF, Connell LJ, Rountree MS, Spinweber CL, Gillen KA (1994) "Crew factors in flight operations IX: Effects of planned cockpit rest on crew performance and alertness in long-haul operations." NASA Technical Memorandum 108839 / DOT/FAA/92/24, NASA Ames Research Center — [NTRS PDF](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/archive/nasa/casi.ntrs.nasa.gov/19950006379.pdf) **[V]**
- **About the "+34% performance / +54% alertness" figures:** these do **not** appear in the NASA memo excerpt that was retrieved. They come from Rosekind's own 2011 statements to the press (CNN, about air-traffic controllers) summarising the study, and from secondary sites such as the Sleep Foundation, which presents them as "up to" increases. They should be cited as "as summarised by the lead author" and not as figures stated in the report. — [Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-hygiene/nasa-nap); [CNN repost via Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/1153158-ntsb-urges-fatigue-fighting-strategies-for-air-traffic-controllers) **[V]**
- Nap length: in a comparison of nap lengths, a 10-min nap gave the quickest and most lasting benefit with minimal sleep inertia (grogginess on waking). Naps of 20–30 min helped but caused inertia first. — Brooks A, Lack L (2006) *Sleep* 29(6):831–840, https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.6.831 **[K]**

**Caffeine**
- 400 mg of caffeine (about 2–3 or more cups of coffee) taken 0, 3 **or 6 h** before bed disrupted sleep significantly. At 6 h before bed it cut objectively measured total sleep time by **more than 1 hour**, yet participants did not notice the loss. The authors advise avoiding substantial caffeine for at least 6 h before bedtime. The study had n=12. — Drake C, Roehrs T, Shambroom J, Roth T (2013) "Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed." *J Clin Sleep Med* 9(11):1195–1200, https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170 — [PMC3805807](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3805807/); [AASM press release](https://aasm.org/late-afternoon-and-early-evening-caffeine-can-disrupt-sleep-at-night/) **[V]**
- Mechanism and limits: caffeine blocks adenosine receptors (see Q1a). It hides sleep pressure without clearing it, so the debt remains, and late doses cut into the next night's sleep, which can start a self-perpetuating cycle (inference). The FDA's general guidance for healthy adults is about 400 mg per day (not verified this session).

**Recovery sleep**
- Weekend catch-up sleep: 36 adults were assigned 9 h nightly, 5 h nightly, or 5 h on weekdays with unlimited weekend sleep. Weekend recovery **did not prevent** metabolic harm. Whole-body insulin sensitivity fell by about 13% with continuous restriction and by about 9–27% in the weekend-recovery group, which also had worse muscle and liver insulin sensitivity. Some short-lived benefits on the weekend, such as less after-dinner snacking, disappeared once restriction resumed. — Depner CM, Melanson EL, Eckel RH, Snell-Bergeon JK, Perreault L, Bergman BC, Higgins JA, Guerin MK, Stothard ER, Morton SJ, Wright KP Jr (2019) "Ad libitum weekend recovery sleep fails to prevent metabolic dysregulation during a repeating pattern of insufficient sleep and weekend recovery sleep." *Curr Biol* 29(6):957–967.e4, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.01.069 — [NIH Research Matters](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/nih-research-matters/weekend-catch-cant-counter-chronic-sleep-deprivation); [CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/today/2019/02/28/catching-sleep-weekend-doesnt-work) **[V]** (effect sizes from news coverage; DOI from prior knowledge)
- Performance recovery: after 7 days of 3–7 h in bed, 3 recovery nights of 8 h did not bring PVT performance back to baseline in the 3–5 h groups. — Belenky G et al. (2003) "Patterns of performance degradation and restoration during sleep restriction and subsequent recovery: a sleep dose-response study." *J Sleep Res* 12(1):1–12, https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2869.2003.00337.x **[K]**
- Total vs chronic loss: total sleep deprivation produced larger deficits relative to the amount of sleep lost. SWS power responded strongly at first and then changed little across 14 restricted nights. — Van Dongen 2003 **[V]**

**Sleep extension**
- 11 Stanford men's basketball players aimed for 10 h in bed for 5–7 weeks and gained about **110.9 min per night** of objective sleep. Sprint times improved by about 4.5% (about 0.7 s), and free-throw and 3-point accuracy each rose by about 9% (9% and 9.2%). Reaction time, daytime sleepiness and mood also improved. The study was small and uncontrolled. — Mah CD, Mah KE, Kezirian EJ, Dement WC (2011) "The effects of sleep extension on the athletic performance of collegiate basketball players." *Sleep* 34(7):943–950, https://doi.org/10.5665/SLEEP.1132 — [Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2011/07/snooze-you-win-its-true-for-achieving-hoop-dreams-says-study.html); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/07/110701083513.htm) **[V]**

**CBT-I and sleep hygiene**
- The AASM 2021 guideline, the first AASM guideline to use GRADE for behavioural insomnia treatments, gives its single **STRONG** recommendation to multicomponent CBT-I: cognitive therapy plus stimulus control, sleep restriction and education. It conditionally recommends brief behavioural therapy, stimulus control alone, sleep restriction alone, and relaxation. It conditionally recommends **against sleep hygiene as a stand-alone therapy**. — Edinger JD, Arnedt JT, Bertisch SM, Carney CE, Harrington JJ, Lichstein KL, Sateia MJ, Troxel WM, Zhou ES, Kazmi U, Heald JL, Martin JL (2021) "Behavioral and psychological treatments for chronic insomnia disorder in adults: an AASM clinical practice guideline." *J Clin Sleep Med* 17(2):255–262, https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986 — [PMC7853203](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/) **[V]**

### Inferences
- What works best: consistent, adequate sleep (≥7 h for adults, 8–10 h for teenagers), a regular schedule, and CBT-I for insomnia.
- What helps temporarily: a 10–30 min nap (keep it short to avoid inertia), and caffeine taken early in the day.
- What does not work: catching up only at weekends (at least for metabolic health), relying on caffeine, assuming you have adapted to short sleep, and using sleep-hygiene tips alone for chronic insomnia.
- Sleep hygiene still has value as a *component* of CBT-I and for prevention. The guideline's recommendation against it applies to its use as the only *treatment* for insomnia disorder.

### Gaps
- The original data behind the NASA "34%/54%" figures were not found in the retrieved memo. They may be in the full report tables (e.g., PVT and EEG/EOG microevent comparisons), which were not readable this session.
- No retrieved study shows that recovery sleep reverses inflammatory, cardiovascular or glymphatic effects in humans.
- The FDA caffeine limit (~400 mg/day) and pregnancy limits (~200 mg/day) were not verified this session.
- Sleep-extension data outside athletes (e.g., blood pressure or appetite in habitually short-sleeping adults) were not retrieved.
